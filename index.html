v4 · A simulation of Meckler's six-stage dynamic symmetrical exchange model · JPIM RF 2026

Who learns fast enough to close the deal?

Four focal firms each trade with a stakeholder in the same market. Both sides build technical (T), emotional (E) and transaction-cost (TC) utility on S-curves, value what they receive against their own floors, and learn from every round. Floors are deal-breakers, and they rise. The installed base and complements grow the same way for everyone; firms differ only in how they allocate and how much they attend to. Every input, moderator and modifier the paper names at each stage interface has a control on the left.

How the model works

One period is one pass through the six stages, with the Stage 4–6 negotiation loop running inside the period. All four firms face identical copies of the same stakeholder and the same shared environment, so differences come only from firm policy.

S-curves. Each dimension's utility is a logistic function of a capability stock: u(x) = L·(σ(k(x−m)) − σ(−km)) / (1 − σ(−km)). T has the highest ceiling and the slowest take-off; E and TC are cheaper but cap lower. Stocks depreciate 3% per period; E decays faster by the E decay setting. The stakeholder's advocacy (E) and cooperation (TC) capabilities use the same curve shapes.

Stage 1. A firm spends up to its per-period budget. Below the triage level it funds only T (the paper's retreat to core survival). Otherwise it first repairs any dimension it believes is below the stakeholder's floor, because a floor breach is a deal-breaker. Then the policy decides the rest. Technical-first puts the rest on T. Balanced splits it three ways. Slope-hunter ranks curves by believed weight × slope, skips any dimension it believes is at the Maximum Demanded level, and invests only when that clears a break-even hurdle of 0.08; otherwise it saves. Orchestrated uses the same rule with full attention, half the signal noise, full attention to the network, and an AI multiplier on its TC curve. Switching the main dimension loses a share of that period's capability gain (pivot friction). When planning, firms see the network's contribution only in proportion to their network attention.

Stage 2. Installed base and complements grow on logistic paths with the same size and speed for every firm, plus a co-evolution increment for every formalized exchange in the market. Complements add to T; the installed base adds to TC and, with the segment's valence, to E (negative for exclusivity segments). Exogenous friction subtracts from TC.

Stage 3. The stakeholder's floors rise each period (capped below the ceilings). Utility above the Maximum Demanded level adds nothing. Human: perceives each dimension with noise, rejects outright if TC is below the friction-abort level, enforces every floor, then requires total value to clear the sum of floors. AI persona: trades off only; no floors, no noise, no abort. Delegated agent: hard floors on T and TC at 110%, emotional weight 0.2, no E floor. Commodity buyer: weights TC heavily and E at zero (the paper's purchasing-algorithm example). Operant resources scale how much T the stakeholder can extract. Value-in-context shocks temporarily move weight from T to TC for every firm.

Stage 4. The stakeholder's dependence is V / (V + alternatives). It opens by paying its believed version of the firm's payment floor plus a share of its remaining surplus that grows with dependence, never more than 90% of the value it assigned (converted to resources at the value-to-resource rate), and never more than its bank. It also invests in advocacy and cooperation when it believes they are below the firm's floors (both sides repair deal-breakers).

Stage 5. Complements augment the cooperation the firm receives (payment rails). The firm reads a noisy signal of the stakeholder's floors, ceilings and weights on the dimensions it attends to; every firm learns which floor broke after a deal-breaker.

Stage 6. The firm's floors are a payment floor (rising) and a cooperation floor. Great clears the payment floor by 30%; OK clears both floors; not good enough triggers renegotiation: the firm states its shortfall, the stakeholder raises payment or cooperation if its surplus and bank allow, and both update their beliefs. If the rounds run out, or the stakeholder rejected in Stage 3, the period ends with no exchange and the firm reinnovates next period. After more consecutive no-deal periods than its reinnovation tolerance, the firm terminates. A formalized exchange pays the firm the payment plus a conversion share of the advocacy and cooperation it received, and adds to the installed base.

Limits. One stakeholder segment per market, no competition between the focal firms for the same stakeholder, and stylized functional forms. It shows how the model's stated logic behaves under different settings. It does not test the theory.
