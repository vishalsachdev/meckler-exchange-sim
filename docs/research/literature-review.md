# Literature review: simulating Meckler's six-stage exchange model

Created: 2026-10-11
Last Updated: 2026-10-11
Scope: supports the single-file simulator at `index.html` (section "How the model works"), built from Mark Meckler's JPIM Research Forum 2026 paper.

**Method.** Every reference below has its metadata (authors, year, venue, volume, pages, DOI) confirmed against the Crossref API or the arXiv API on 2026-10-11. For most entries I also read the abstract, from Crossref, arXiv, or a search result that quoted it. Some entries are marked *[abstract not opened]*. For those, Crossref confirmed the metadata but returned no abstract, so the one-line summary relies on the source's well-known content. Check those before you quote them. Working papers and preprints are labelled as such.

**What the simulator assumes, in short.** Four firms with different policies invest in technical (T), emotional (E) and transaction-cost (TC) utility. Each dimension follows a logistic curve. One stakeholder values each firm's package against Minimum Necessary floors that rise over time and Maximum Demanded ceilings. There are four evaluator types: a noisy human who screens on floors and can abort, a "persona" that trades off only, a delegated AI agent with hard T/TC floors and little weight on E, and a commodity buyer. Inside each period, a two-sided loop of offers and renegotiation ends in formalization, reinnovation or termination. The installed base and complements are shared. Firms differ in attention budget, network attention, signal noise and learning rate. The orchestrated firm has full attention, less noise and a steeper TC curve.

## (a) Five concrete suggestions

1. **Make the AI evaluators evidence-based rather than idealized.** The persona currently has "no floors, no noise" and the delegated agent has "hard floors". Replace these with parameters taken from the evidence:
   - persona decision rule: compensatory or lexicographic (Goli & Singh 2024);
   - persona variance: lower than human, plus a run-level prompt effect (Bisbee et al. 2024);
   - delegated-agent biases: position and first-proposal bias (Allouah et al. 2025; Bansal et al. 2025);
   - floor-violation probability (Zhu et al. 2025);
   - a "model update" shock that redraws agent weights (Allouah et al. 2025).

   Then the human / persona / agent comparison becomes a finding, not a built-in assumption.
2. **Add interdependence among T, E and TC.** Make utility non-separable, for example E and TC payoffs gated by T clearing its floor, or an NK-style interaction term (Levinthal 1997; Lenox et al. 2006). Pivot friction then becomes one form of path dependence among several. Slope-hunting and orchestration should also separate more clearly, because a rugged landscape is where better representation pays (Gavetti & Levinthal 2000).
3. **Formalize attention along Ocasio's three principles.** Keep the budget (*focus*). Add context-dependent noticing, so a context shock or floor breach captures attention (*situated* attention; *vigilance*, Ocasio 2011). Add channels: network attention as a structural channel, not one scalar. Let absorptive capacity grow with past investment in a dimension (Cohen & Levinthal 1990). No ABV agent-based model turned up in the search (stream 3, "Gap found"), so this could be the joint paper's theoretical contribution, not just a feature.
4. **Report hot-stove and dynamic-environment diagnostics.** For terminated firms, compute the counterfactual: would they have formalized if their tolerance were higher (Denrell & March 2001)? Sweep floor-rise speed against learning rate to test whether faster-rising floors call for more or less exploration (Posen & Levinthal 2012). These outputs turn the simulator into theory elaboration in the Davis et al. (2007) sense.
5. **Triangulate with real agents and real people.** A three-leg validation for a joint paper with Meckler:
   - (i) run the stylized simulator;
   - (ii) put live LLM agents in the stakeholder (and optionally firm) roles, inside an existing testbed such as Magentic Marketplace or AgenticPay, following Tranchero et al. (2026);
   - (iii) run a small human lab study of the Stage 3 floor-then-trade-off rule, following Billinger et al. (2014).

   Where the three legs agree, the model's claims are robust. Where they disagree, you have the paper's boundary conditions (Burton & Obel 2011).
